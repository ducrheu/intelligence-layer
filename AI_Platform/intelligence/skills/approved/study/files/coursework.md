# Coursework: Assignments, Essays, Labs, Group Work, Thesis

Coursework is graded on a rubric that is almost always published and almost never read. It is also where academic-integrity rules bite, and where the agent's boundary is hardest and least negotiable.

**Before any coursework session**, read `## Courses` for weights and hurdles and `## Results` for where the grade currently stands. A thesis, capstone or graded group project is tracked in the shared `~/Clawic/data/projects/<project>.md`, not in the study box (`memory-template.md`).

## Read the Rubric Before Writing

Most coursework marks are lost against criteria the student never read.

1. **Get the rubric and the marking criteria** — they are usually in the handbook or the assignment brief.
2. **Convert each criterion into a question** the finished work must answer, in writing. "Critical evaluation, 30%" → "where do I say what is wrong with the position I am defending?"
3. **Allocate effort by criterion weight.** A 30% criterion deserves roughly 30% of the hours; polishing the introduction is rarely one of them.
4. **Self-mark the draft against the rubric** before submitting, criterion by criterion, with a number. This is the single highest-yield hour in any assignment.
5. Where an exemplar of a high-scoring answer exists, read it against the rubric to see what the criteria actually look like when met.

## The Deliverable-First Plan

Work backwards from the artifact, not forwards from the reading:

- **Write the skeleton on day one**: sections, the claim of each, the evidence each needs. It converts an unbounded reading task into a bounded list of gaps.
- **Time-box the research.** Reading for an assignment expands to fill whatever is available; the skeleton is what says when to stop.
- **Draft badly and early.** A complete bad draft is editable; three perfect paragraphs on day six are not.
- **Reserve the last 15% of the time for the rubric self-mark, references and formatting.** These are cheap marks and they are the ones lost to time pressure.
- Two deadlines on the same day is one deadline you will miss — pull the movable one forward (SKILL.md, Deadlines And Grade Math).

## Essays and Written Assignments

- **Answer the question asked.** Underline the command word (evaluate, compare, to what extent, critically assess) and the constraint. Off-question quality does not score.
- **The thesis goes in the first paragraph** and every section connects back to it explicitly. Markers reading forty scripts reward a visible argument.
- **Evidence density is the differentiator** between a good and an excellent mark: specific sources, dates, data, cases — not more adjectives.
- **The counter-argument is where the top band lives.** State the strongest objection and answer it; a one-sided essay caps out.
- **Reference as you write**, in the required style. Reconstructing citations afterwards costs hours and produces errors that read as sloppiness.
- **Read it aloud once** before submitting: it catches the sentence-level problems no reread does.
- Word limits are constraints with penalties. Cutting 15% almost always improves the piece.

## Problem Sets

- **Attempt before collaborating**, and before any hint (SKILL.md Rule 8). A problem set you watched someone solve is a lecture.
- **Show the method and the reasoning**: marks are usually awarded for the approach, so a wrong final number with a correct method scores far above a bare answer.
- **Problem sets are the term's best exam preparation** — do them under something like exam time once the material is acquired (`subjects.md`).
- Keep the returned marked set. The corrections are the highest-quality feedback available in most courses, and they belong in `errors.md` with their causes.
- Where collaboration is permitted, the rule is usually "discuss the approach, write independently". Know the exact wording before working with anyone.

## Lab Reports

- **The reasoning is the grade**, not the result. An experiment that produced a poor number, well analysed, outscores a clean number with no error analysis.
- **Record everything at the bench in the lab notebook, including what went wrong.** The methods and discussion sections are unwritable from memory a week later.
- **Error analysis and uncertainty are the differentiating sections** in most schemes, and the most often rushed.
- **The discussion answers a question**: what does this result mean, and what would change the conclusion? A restatement of the results is not a discussion.
- Follow the required structure exactly — lab reports are marked against a fixed template and deviations lose marks mechanically.
- A procedure or analysis template that worked belongs in `artifacts/` and saves hours on every subsequent report.

## Presentations

- **Time it aloud, twice.** Over-running is the most common and most penalized failure, and silent rehearsal always under-estimates duration.
- **One idea per slide**, and no slide the audience must read while you talk — they will read instead of listening.
- **Prepare for the questions specifically**: the three most likely, plus the one you hope nobody asks. The Q&A carries real marks in most rubrics.
- Know the first sentence and the last sentence verbatim (`subjects.md`, verbatim material). The middle can be extemporized; the edges cannot.
- Check the equipment and the file format beforehand. Technical failure is not usually accepted as an excuse.

## Group Projects

- **Agree the deliverable, the split and the internal deadlines in writing at the first meeting**, and put them in the shared project file. Ambiguity at the start is what produces the crisis at the end.
- **Internal deadlines are set a week before the real one.** The buffer is the whole design.
- **Integration is a task with an owner and a slot**, not something that happens at the end. Most group failures are integration failures, not effort failures.
- **A non-contributing member is escalated early, in writing, to the tutor.** Waiting until the deadline leaves no remedy and no evidence.
- Where the mark is individual, keep a record of your own contributions as you go in `projects/<project>.md` — reconstructing it afterwards is unconvincing.
- Teammates and the supervising tutor go in the shared `contacts.md`; the project itself in `projects/<project>.md` (`memory-template.md`).

## Thesis and Dissertation

- **The research question is the whole project.** A question too broad produces a year of unfocused reading; narrow it until it is answerable with the resources you have.
- **Write from month one.** A literature review drafted while reading is a chapter; a literature review deferred to the end is a crisis.
- **Ethics approval and data access are long-lead items with fixed calendars** and they block everything downstream. They go in the milestone table on day one.
- **Supervisor meetings need an agenda and a written follow-up**: what was decided, what is next, by when. A year of undocumented meetings produces disagreement about what was agreed (`groups.md`).
- **Decisions with their reasoning go in the project file** — the design chosen, the ones rejected, and why. The defence asks exactly these (`exams.md`, vivas).
- **The limitations section is examined harder than the results.** Write it honestly and early.
- Backups on a cadence, in `## Due`, with at least one copy off the machine. Thesis loss is catastrophic and entirely preventable.
- Milestones, decisions and status go in the shared `projects/<project>.md`; the topic states and reading stay in the study box.

## Deadlines, Extensions and Late Penalties

- **Compute the trade explicitly.** A 10%-per-day penalty against a piece you could improve 5% by working one more day is a losing trade — submit.
- **Ask for an extension before the week it is due**, with the reason and a proposed new date. Requests on the deadline day are usually refused and always look worse.
- **Know the institution's process**: self-certification windows, medical evidence, mitigating-circumstances panels. These have their own deadlines, which people miss while dealing with the reason they needed them.
- **Submit something at the deadline even if incomplete.** A partial submission scores; a missing one usually scores zero and can trigger a hurdle failure.
- Check the submission mechanics early — file format, size limits, plagiarism-checker processing time, the portal's clock versus yours. The portal's timezone is the one that counts, and it goes in `config.yaml` under `platform`.

## Academic Integrity

The boundary is `integrity_mode`, default `scaffold`, and it does not bend for deadline pressure.

- **The agent never produces text, code, proofs or analysis that will be submitted for credit.** It asks, hints, critiques, and checks against the rubric (SKILL.md Rule 8).
- **What is always safe**: explaining a concept, quizzing, critiquing the student's own draft against the rubric, checking a citation format, generating practice questions, planning the work.
- **What depends on the course's stated rules**: AI assistance of any kind, collaboration on problem sets, using solutions manuals, sharing notes and past work. Record the course's actual policy in `config.yaml` under the integrity area and follow it.
- **Cite AI use where the institution requires it.** Undisclosed use that the policy required disclosing is the offence, and it is the common case.
- **Plagiarism is mostly accidental**: paraphrase too close to the source, a missing citation, or self-plagiarism from a previous submission. Reference while writing and the risk largely disappears.
- Contract cheating and buying work end degrees. There is no version of this the pressure justifies, and a missed deadline is recoverable in a way that an integrity finding is not.
- If the student is asking for the work to be produced because they cannot start, that is a different problem with a different fix (`motivation.md`).

**When coursework is planned, drafted, submitted or returned**, write the mark and weight to `## Results` with the recomputed target, every miss the feedback exposes to `errors.md`, and the deadline outcomes to `## Courses`. A rubric-mapped skeleton, a report template or a working structure goes to `artifacts/<kebab-name>.md` with its `## Boxes` line; a thesis, capstone or graded group project keeps its goal, milestones and decisions in the shared `projects/<project>.md` and its people in `contacts.md` (`memory-template.md`).
